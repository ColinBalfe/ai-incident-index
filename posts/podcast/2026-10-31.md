**THE WARNING**
Chainalysis says it was broken into, and it says the attacker used AI agents to do it.

Chainalysis makes the software that law-enforcement agencies, tax authorities and crypto exchanges use to follow stolen money on the blockchain. On Friday it sent its customers a notice. According to FinanceFeeds, which quotes that notice, an outside party got in, "an external actor using offensive agentic techniques." Agentic means AI that carries out a chain of steps on its own.

What was taken: customers' user IDs, full names, email addresses and the role labels that say what each person does at their organization. According to FinanceFeeds, Chainalysis says it has no evidence so far that investigation files, passwords, two-factor secrets or login sessions were reached. CoinGape quotes the company: "We've proactively notified all of our customers." And Chainalysis says this has nothing to do with this week's giant Binance theft.

Plain context: this is a company whose whole job is catching criminals online. Its customers are the people doing the catching.

The caveat, and it's a big one. Chainalysis hasn't named the attacker. It hasn't said which AI, or how the agents were used, or how many customers were hit. It says it serves more than 1,500 clients. The AI part is the company's own description. Nobody independent has backed it up yet. And we couldn't open the notice itself this morning; we're relying on three outlets that quote it.

So the real question is: if the AI label holds, this is a named company confirming an AI-agent break-in. Days earlier, the UK web host 123 Reg said an "AI-driven cyberattack" read its customers' data. That's two in a week.

**What we know:** a named company says AI-driven attackers got in and took names, emails and roles.
**What we don't know:** who did it, which AI, how many people.
**What would change this:** an outside investigator confirming the AI role, or a count.

**Our call:** expect more companies to describe break-ins this way over the next month. Some of that will be real, and some will be a convenient label; we'll grade them one by one.

**What it means for you:** unless you use Chainalysis at work, this didn't touch you. But here's the lesson that travels. After a breach like this, the danger is the email that knows your name and your job title. One thing to do today: if a message knows who you are and asks you to log in or pay, check it another way. Call the person. Type the address yourself.

**DAY 11: THE FLAW UNDER MOST WEBSITES**
Day eleven of PHP2Shell, the flaw in PHP, the software under most websites.

This one is still **Proven**: test systems fell, no real victim confirmed by name.

What changed: according to Cybersecurity Dive, Palo Alto Networks has confirmed that a suspected state-linked actor is using chained exploits against a small number of customer firewalls. A spokesperson put the share of its installed base that was exposed to the internet and potentially affected at about 0.02 percent. Still, no customer named. And no organization has stood up and said "it was us."

The third flaw still has no fix. The next dated test: by November 7th, will a real organization confirm it was broken into through this flaw? Our odds, unchanged: 70 percent.

**THE BREAKTHROUGH**
From this week's record. A Mayo Clinic study in the journal Gut found its AI flagged 73 percent of pancreatic cancers on CT scans that radiologists had read as clear. The radiologists, looking at the same kind of scans, caught 38.9 percent. The median lead time: 475 days before diagnosis.

To be clear, we corrected this one on Thursday: that's a median, not an average, and the 73 percent was measured on scans held back for testing. It's a study, not a tool your doctor has. But pancreatic cancer kills because it's found late. More than a year earlier is the whole game.

**YOUR LIFE**
*Your job.* Comcast filed notices cutting 912 jobs: 634 in Philadelphia, 119 in New Jersey, 159 in Washington state, effective December 31st, according to The Philadelphia Inquirer. Executives say they're expanding AI agents to handle customer service. To be clear, the notices themselves don't cite AI, and the roles cut are engineers, managers and analysts. Comcast also plans more than 1,100 new hires. Takeaway: the cuts and the AI plans arrive together; read the notice, not the press release.

*Your money.* The Chainalysis lesson again, because it's yours too. A breach of names and emails is a phishing kit. Takeaway: the more a message knows about you, the more you check it.

*Your kids.* In Kansas, the Shawnee Mission School District has a 32-person group, mostly parents, drafting its rules for students using AI, according to the Johnson County Post. The district is waiting on the state board's own policy, likely up in November. Right now, no generative AI is on district-issued devices. Takeaway: these rules are being written this fall. If you're a parent, ask to be in the room.
