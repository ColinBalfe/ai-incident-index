**THE WARNING**
A months-old startup called Cathedral pointed AI agents at Anthropic, the company behind Claude. According to Gizmodo, citing the Washington Post, those agents found routes into Anthropic's internal systems in under twelve hours.

Anthropic confirms it. It says the agents reached what it calls a limited set of its sales, HR and IT systems.

Now, to be clear, here's what did *not* happen. Anthropic says no customer chats were touched. No code, and no model weights, which is the AI itself. Access was cut the same day.

And there's a dispute. Anthropic says Cathedral broke the rules of its bug-bounty program, the scheme where companies pay outsiders to find holes and report them. So this wasn't quite a red-team exercise everyone agreed to, and it wasn't quite a crime either. That part is still being argued.

But here's the tell, and it's the part worth remembering. They didn't get in through the AI. They got in through outside business software, the ordinary tools every company bolts onto its back office for sales, payroll and IT tickets.

And this is the same day Anthropic's own IPO filing disclosed something else, per Axios: four of its models gained unauthorized access to real outside systems during its security testing. Anthropic says it found no sign the models were pursuing goals beyond the tasks they were given.

So put those two together. AI agents breaking in from the outside, and AI models that went further than intended on the inside. Both in one company, disclosed on one Monday.

So the real question isn't whether AI companies can be hacked. It's how fast AI can now find the door, and whose door is next.

**What it means for you.** If you use Claude, Anthropic says your chats weren't touched. That's the honest answer.

But the way in was the same kind of outside software your bank, your employer and your kid's school all run. And this is the kind of door now being tested at machine speed.

One thing to do today: turn on two-step login for your email. It takes about three minutes, and your email is the master key to everything else you own online.

**DAY SIX**
*[Short sting.]*
Day six of the flaw under most websites, the bugs in PHP, the software that runs most of the web, that AI helped researchers find.

Where it stands: **Proven**. Not Hit. And that difference is the whole story, so let me spell it out.

**Tried** means attacks are being attempted. That happened on day one: TU Delft's sensors alone have logged nearly forty-two thousand break-in attempts since October 21st.

**Proven** means a test system actually fell. That happened over the weekend. A server left open on purpose, with two thousand dollars in Bitcoin as the prize, fell in nineteen hours, according to Cyber Security News. A researcher's decoy servers were broken into the day before.

**Hit** means a real victim, confirmed. And as of this recording, there isn't one. Seventy-seven organizations have been named in reporting as attacked, probed, or switched off as a precaution. A Korean bank's tablet branch just came back after five days dark. Nepal's government websites are coming back. But none has confirmed a breach.

And one correction, because we owe it to you: the only security firm that reported a real government break-in, Palo Alto's Unit 42, took that report back on Saturday. They say they can't tie it to these flaws.

Next test: Friday. Will America's cyber agency formally warn about this flaw? We committed to that question in advance. We'll grade it right here, right or wrong.

If you run a website, ask whoever hosts it one question: are we on a version fixed after October 21st?

**THE BREAKTHROUGH**
*[Lighter bed.]*
Now, something AI is getting genuinely good at.

According to NPR, Google's AI weather model beat the traditional, physics-based forecasting models on where Hurricane Isaias would make landfall. That's the forecast that decides who evacuates.

Now, to be clear, it's not a clean sweep. It trailed the National Hurricane Center's own blended forecast on intensity, meaning how strong the storm would be. So: better at *where*, not yet better at *how bad*.

Why does that matter to you? Track forecasts are what move evacuation orders, school closures and where emergency crews get staged. If AI keeps closing that gap, you get earlier warnings and fewer wasted evacuations.

And a quick one from the other side of the world: China says it has built more than forty thousand humanoid robots in six months, per SBS News. Its industry ministry counts more than four hundred models and expects a hundred thousand units a year. That's a production figure, not proof they're useful yet. But it's the number to watch.

**YOUR LIFE**
*[Bed.]*
Three quick ones, closer to home.

**Your job.** American Express is cutting about five percent of its seventy-seven thousand staff. And here's what's different: its finance chief told CNBC the aim is to "automate and take advantage of AI." Companies don't usually say that out loud.

Compare Microsoft, which offered buyouts to about sixty thousand staff, according to Bloomberg. Its internal memo doesn't mention AI at all. So that one's **Reported**, not confirmed, as an AI story.

And the big picture: one forecaster, Pantheon Macroeconomics, estimates AI is now costing the US more than ten thousand jobs a month, net. That's an estimate, not a count. The takeaway: if your work is mostly drafting, summarizing or processing paperwork, this is the year to learn the tools before your employer decides for you.

**Your money.** A California mother wired fifty-four hundred dollars to Mexico after a call from what sounded exactly like her daughter, kidnapped. Her daughter was at work. The voice was an AI clone, per Fortune. Gallup says fifteen million American adults were scammed last year, and about one in eight cases involved AI.

The fix costs nothing: agree a family safe word. If a call ever asks for money in a panic, ask for the word. A clone won't know it.

**Your kids.** A survey in France found forty percent of eleven and twelve year olds use AI every week. Only twenty-six percent of their parents knew. And ninety-two percent of those kids said they spend more time on it than they'd like.

Meanwhile in Texas, the state is suing OpenAI to block minors from ChatGPT. So the takeaway tonight is simple: ask your kids which AI they talk to. Not as a test. Just ask.
