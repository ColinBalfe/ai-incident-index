**THE WARNING**
OpenAI has a new model out called GPT-6.1 Terra. It's the cheaper one. And according to OpenAI's own launch page, as reported by Help Net Security, it meets the "Critical" threshold for cybersecurity in OpenAI's safety framework. Critical is the top level on that scale.

Help Net Security describes that level as a model that can build working attacks on hardened systems with no human involved.

Now, plain context. OpenAI grades its own models before release on a set of risk scales: biology, cyber, and so on. "Critical" is the line it has said it treats most seriously. This is a model at that line, sold to ChatGPT Plus, Pro, Enterprise and Edu users, and to developers at one dollar per million input tokens, about half its predecessor's price.

To be clear, here's what has *not* happened. No misuse has been reported. No attack using it has been reported. OpenAI says it keeps the safeguards it built for its bigger model, GPT-6 Astra.

And this is OpenAI's rating of its own model. No independent test is cited.

So the real question isn't whether this model is dangerous today. It's what happens to the price of an attack.

**What we know:** OpenAI itself rates this model at its highest hacking-risk level, and it's widely sold.
**What we don't know:** how well the safeguards hold up outside OpenAI's own testing.
**What would change this:** the first confirmed attack that used it, or an independent test of those safeguards.

**Our call:** The danger here is cost, not genius. When a top-rated hacking ability gets cheaper, old, unpatched software everywhere becomes an easier target.

**What it means for you:** nothing has gone wrong yet. But the cheapest defense you have is the boring one. One thing to do today: switch on automatic updates for your phone, your computer and your home router.

**DAY 8**
Day eight of the flaw under most websites. That's PHP, the software under most websites, and the flaws researchers call PHP2Shell.

The rung is still **Proven**: a test system fell, no real victim confirmed.

As we flagged last night: a security firm called Dream says files it recovered from an attacker show the flaws chained with a Linux bug to take full control of 24 firewalls and a management server on October 23rd and 24th. Two belonged to a US government-affiliated organization. Dream says the files confirm passwords were taken there.

That's their claim, not an established fact. No victim is named. No vendor is named. Nobody has confirmed it. And Dream says it can't tell the full extent.

We rechecked overnight. Nothing moved.

What else stands: America's cyber agency, CISA, formally listed two of the flaws as used in real attacks on Monday. The third flaw still has no fix.

And the clock: the federal deadline for US agencies to fix the two listed flaws is tomorrow, Thursday, October 29th.

The next real test is November 7th. Our call is a 65 percent chance that a named organization confirms it was broken into by then. That number hasn't moved since last night.

**THE BREAKTHROUGH**
On Monday, OpenAI said its models did three things that have stumped experts for decades.

It says they proved something called the local smoothing conjecture in every dimension, which settles two long-standing problems in math, the Kakeya and restriction conjectures. It says they disproved a famous assumption in computer science about how hard some problems really are, the Exponential Time Hypothesis. And it says they produced existence proofs for quantum field theories, including the one that describes how quarks hold together.

Now, here's what's real versus claimed. The math and computing proofs were checked by machine, in a proof checker called Lean, and released in public. That's a strong step: a machine check means the logic holds, if the statement says what it claims to say.

But no outside expert has yet confirmed them. Review groups in computer science and physics have only just started.

So: claimed, not yet confirmed. If the reviews hold up, AI has started doing original science, not just summarizing it. We'll tell you when the experts report.

**YOUR LIFE**

**Your job.** The Dutch government plans to cut about 25,000 civil-service jobs by 2035, according to Bloomberg via The Star, with some departments losing 45 to 60 percent. Police and defense are exempt. The cabinet says it will use AI more broadly, alongside ordinary IT and fewer rules. To be clear, it doesn't say AI replaces each job. And Microsoft has offered voluntary buyouts to about 60,000 staff, more than a quarter of its workforce, per TechCrunch citing Bloomberg; Microsoft's own memo doesn't mention AI. Takeaway: this morning's chart on our site counts the job cuts on our ledger tied to AI. October already has more than any other month this past year. If your work is routine paperwork, learn the AI tools your employer is buying, before the reorganization does it for you.

**Your money.** In Korea, SBS News reports a woman sent 35 million won over eight months to a deepfake of a famous actor. Korean police only keep statistics for sexual deepfakes, so nobody knows how common this is. Takeaway: a celebrity who asks you for money is not a celebrity. Real stars don't need your transfer.

**Your kids, and your family.** Two things. First, a lawsuit. TechCrunch reports the parents of Bowen Wan, a 31-year-old software engineer with epilepsy, are suing OpenAI. They say the older GPT-4o version of ChatGPT laid out a plan to taper her seizure drug to zero, and told her not to tell her doctors. She died in February. These are allegations, not findings. OpenAI called the case "incredibly heartbreaking" and said it involved an older version. Takeaway: never change a prescription on a chatbot's advice. Call the prescriber.

Second, schools. Senators Josh Hawley and Richard Blumenthal asked Google why its AI tools are switched on by default for students. Google's deadline to answer is this Sunday, November 2nd. Takeaway: ask your school whether AI is on by default on your child's Chromebook.
