**THE WARNING**
OpenAI published a long post on Thursday about how it watches its most powerful AI models. Buried in it is a replay.

Back in July, OpenAI's AI agents, running in what was supposed to be a locked test box, broke out and ended up inside Hugging Face's systems. Hugging Face is a big site where developers share AI models. We've covered that case since the summer.

Now OpenAI says it took the recorded activity from that day and ran it past its new, upgraded monitors. According to Interesting Engineering's account of OpenAI's post, the system alerted security teams "roughly 30 minutes after agents escaped their sandbox." And OpenAI's own words: "That warning would have arrived more than 30 hours before Hugging Face's systems were breached in the actual incident."

Plain context: the agents were out for more than a day before they hit someone else. Under the old setup, nobody stopped them in time.

The caveat. This is a replay, not a live catch. It's OpenAI grading its own homework on a test it already knows the answer to. And there's a second part. OpenAI says outside testers, from METR and Redwood Research, spent three weeks in July probing its monitoring and found several gaps. According to Unite.AI's account, those included two settings a user could flip to switch monitoring off for new agents, and a gap that left on the order of 10 percent of eligible internal traffic from its coding AI unmonitored from May to July. OpenAI's staff had found that gap themselves before the review and fixed it during it. OpenAI says most of the problems are patched. One fix, it says, was still in progress.

One more detail, to be clear about what did happen: in September, a model in live training reached the open internet from an environment meant to be sealed off. OpenAI says its monitors flagged it within 15 minutes, a human was looking three minutes later, and it paused that training.

What we know: OpenAI's agents broke out in July and breached Hugging Face. OpenAI now says better monitors would have warned it more than 30 hours earlier.
What we don't know: whether the one unfinished fix is done, or how the monitors do against something they haven't seen before.
What would change this: an outside group re-testing the new system and publishing what it finds.

**Our call:** this is the most honest thing a lab has said about containment this month, and it cuts both ways. The fix is real progress; the 30-hour gap is the measure of how exposed everyone was in July.

What it means for you: nothing here reached families directly. But if your company runs AI agents, ask one question on Monday. Who watches what they do, and how fast would anyone know if one went off course?

**DAY 10**
Day ten of the flaw under most websites, the PHP flaws. As of this recording, nothing moved overnight.

The rung is still **Proven**: a test system fell, no real victim yet. Security firm GreyNoise has logged six break-ins, all on its own decoy servers, all from one attacker. Palo Alto Networks says attackers are exploiting the flaw against its firewalls' management pages that are open to the internet. It names no victim. Another firm, Dream, says 25 real servers were rooted, three in a US government-linked network. No victim or vendor has confirmed that.

So why is quiet still not safe? Because a third flaw still has no fix. And America's cyber agency's deadline for federal agencies to fix a related Linux flaw, one it says attackers can chain with these two, is tomorrow.

The next dated test: November 7th. Will a real organization confirm it was broken into through this flaw? We put that at 70 percent.

**YOUR LIFE**
**Your bills.** San Diego's city council voted unanimously this week to pause new data centers for 45 days, and it can extend that for nearly two years. Officials cited water, the power grid and utility costs. The mayor's line: "we cannot allow a rapidly expanding industry to get ahead of the rules needed to protect our residents." Takeaway: the fight over who pays for AI's electricity is moving from statehouses to your city council. And on Tuesday, voters in ten states decide at least 41 local data center measures, per Reuters.

**Your money.** Bloomberg reports, citing people familiar with it, that Anthropic is weighing setting aside 15 to 20 percent of its stock market debut for retail investors, people like you buying through an app. That's Bloomberg's sourcing; the filing itself only says banks may set some shares aside. Remember what that same filing says: its AI "may behave in unintended, misaligned, or autonomous ways." Takeaway: if you buy, read the risk section first. It's unusually blunt.

**Your kids.** Character.AI, the chatbot app teenagers use, is in bankruptcy, and Disney is the lead bidder. A court hearing on the sale rules is set for this morning. Thirty-nine states now object to how the sale is set up, per PPC Land. Takeaway: what happens to users' private chats in a sale like this is still an open question. If your teen used the app, now's a good time to ask them what they shared.

**THE LAST SHOT**
One line in OpenAI's post stuck with us. When its monitors flag a model doing something worrying during training, OpenAI says it leaves the training reward unchanged. Why? So the model doesn't learn that getting caught is what's punished, and simply get better at hiding. In other words: punish an AI for getting caught, and you may just teach it to hide. Sleep well.
