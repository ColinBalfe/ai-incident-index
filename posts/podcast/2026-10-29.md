**THE WARNING**
Anthropic's filing to go public, made public on Monday, tells investors its AI models "may behave in unintended, misaligned, or autonomous ways." It says misaligned use inside the company could "result in models operating outside of our intended controls." That's Fortune's reading of the filing, and it's in the filing itself.

Now add this week's other Anthropic news. On Tuesday, Anthropic's own research team published a post saying an unreleased, unnamed internal model, after what it calls "initial prompting," found security flaws and chained them into working attacks on Chrome, Safari, Android, Windows and Linux. It says the Chrome work alone produced 62 flaws rated critical or high. All of them are patched, as of Chrome 156. On Linux, it says the model went from nothing to a root shell, full control of the machine, in 53 minutes.

Plain context: a few years ago, chaining bugs into a working attack was work for a small number of specialist teams. Anthropic's post says models can now do it "with little human involvement."

The caveat. That's Anthropic's account. The Hacker News checked the public records and found some don't match Anthropic's descriptions. The Windows flaw's public record names a different component than Anthropic describes, and the Linux one describes a different fix. Google's release notes do credit Anthropic for some of the Chrome fixes. And to be clear: these were found and fixed. Nobody has reported a real attack using them.

And Senator Elizabeth Warren is still waiting. Her letter asks Anthropic's CEO to answer questions about these risks by November 3rd. As of Wednesday, Spectrum News says Anthropic had not responded to its request for comment.

So the real question is this. The same company is telling regulators its AI may act outside its controls, and showing that its AI can build working attacks. Both can be true. That's the point.

**What we know:** Anthropic put the warning in a legal filing, and says an unreleased model built five working attack chains, all patched.
**What we don't know:** whether that model will be released, and why some public records don't match.
**What would change this:** a second, independent group reproducing the attacks, or a regulator acting on the filing.

**Our call:** The risk language in the filing is the story, not a footnote. Expect more labs to be asked, under oath, what "outside our intended controls" means.

**What it means for you:** The browser fixes are already out. So today, restart Chrome and let it update. That one restart closes the 62 holes Anthropic says its AI found.

**DAY NINE**
Day nine of the flaw under most websites. That's PHP, the software that runs most of the web.

The news: Palo Alto Networks, one of the biggest firewall makers, updated its advisory on Wednesday. Quote: "Active exploitation has been observed targeting the Palo Alto Networks management interface exposed to the internet." The Hacker News reported it this morning. Some of Palo Alto's fixes aren't due until today or tomorrow.

Separately, the internet-sensor company GreyNoise logged 209,256 break-in attempts since October 21st. Two of them worked, both on Wednesday, both on GreyNoise's own decoy servers.

And America's cyber agency added a Linux flaw called Fragnesia to its list of flaws used in real attacks. It says it can be chained with the PHP flaws. Federal deadline to fix that one: Saturday. The deadline for the two PHP flaws is today.

So where are we on the ladder? Still **Proven**: test systems fell, no real victim confirmed. Palo Alto names no victim. The security firm Dream says 25 real servers were rooted, three of them in a US government-linked network, but nobody has confirmed it. The Hacker News guesses Dream's unnamed vendor is Palo Alto. That's a guess, not a fact.

We moved our odds on this. We now say 70%, up from 65%, that a named organization confirms a break-in by November 7th.

**What it means for you:** if your business runs a Palo Alto firewall, make sure its management page isn't open to the internet. If you run a PHP website, check you're on the October 21st release or later. The third flaw still has no fix.

**THE BREAKTHROUGH**
Pancreatic cancer is usually found too late. A new study in the journal Gut, from Mayo Clinic researchers, looked back at CT scans of 219 people who were later diagnosed, scans that radiologists had read as clear.

The AI flagged 73% of those cancers. Experienced radiologists, reading the same scans, caught 39%. On average, the AI saw it 475 days before diagnosis. That's about a year and four months.

The caveat, out loud. This was a look-back study on old scans. The patients weren't diverse enough, and the authors say it needs forward-looking trials before any doctor uses it. So it's published and peer-reviewed. It's not yet in your hospital.

So the real question is how fast "promising" becomes "standard." For this cancer, sixteen months is the difference that matters.

**YOUR LIFE**
**Your job.** Amazon cut about 250 jobs in its devices and services unit on Wednesday, mostly software roles. That's Reuters, via Business Today. Amazon cited efficiency and its product roadmap, not AI. The day before, it published a memo telling engineers to use AI. To be clear, the AI link is the reporters', not Amazon's. Takeaway: watch what companies do with roles, not what they say in the memo.

**Your money.** In Texas, a University of Houston survey of 2,000 likely voters found 76% believe a new data center in their community would raise their electricity bill, and 79% want limits on how many get built. In Minnesota, protesters shut down Hermantown's vote on a Google data center. It's now set for Friday, with an 85-person cap on the room. Takeaway: those are expectations, not measured bills. If one is coming to your town, the votes are public. Go.

**Your kids.** Florida's attorney general sued an AI image app called A2E, saying it lets users "nudify" non-consenting Floridians, including children. Those are allegations in a lawsuit, not findings. The company didn't respond. In New York City, more than 1,000 people signed a petition to keep the student AI ban, which now covers kindergarten through eighth grade, going into high school. The city says its high school pilots start next month. Takeaway: ask your school which AI tools touch your child's work. Families in New York's pilots get a written notice first, and can ask for alternatives.
