Since late September a wave of breaches has hit internet-facing systems at South Korean banks, savings banks and fintechs. CrowdStrike says ARTEX, an open-source AI hacking agent running mainly on DeepSeek, was likely behind the bank attacks, and profiles the operator as a financially motivated Chinese speaker who also used Claude Code. Regulators put exposure across seven firms at about 68,000 records; President Lee ordered a full investigation.

Damage

About 68,000 records exposed across seven financial firms (largest: Yegaram Savings ~40,000, Shinhan ~25,000); more banks attacked but blocked

Inside the incident

How it happened

The targets were secondary, externally exposed systems — a loan-broker status site, mobile staff tools, sales-support apps — rather than core banking. The agent mostly reused known techniques, at machine speed, against many institutions at once. Woori and NH Nonghyup were hit with the same methods, but their intrusion-prevention and access controls stopped them.

The AI’s role

AI multiplied one person's reach rather than inventing anything new. CrowdStrike says the ARTEX instance ran mainly on DeepSeek v4.1-flash, with GLM-5.3 and Grok 4.6 in later sessions; the actor also used Claude Code and asked Claude questions, such as where Korean breach data is sold. On Oct 4 Korea's Financial Security Institute said the savings-bank attacks came from different IPs; by Oct 12 regulators reported the same attacker IPs across all seven affected firms, though the FSS cautions that IP-based attribution can mislead.

Damage so far

The largest leak was at Yegaram Savings Bank (an estimated 40,000 people), followed by Shinhan (about 25,000 customers, including some resident registration numbers). Smaller leaks hit KB Kookmin (119 customers), Hana (89), Welcome Savings (about 2,200 corporate-client records), Hyundai Capital, BNK Busan and two fintechs. Regulators put combined exposure across seven firms at about 68,000 records and say it may rise. Hana says its core account and transaction systems were unaffected.

Why it matters

It shows what AI-scaled crime looks like in practice: no new exploit, just one operator covering far more ground. Seoul's response — an emergency regulator meeting, a sector-wide highest alert and a presidential order to investigate — is a template other financial regulators may follow.

What it means next

A human adversary leveraging AI agents can run widespread attacks: it 'allows one human to target many customers in a very short period of time using the power of AI.'Adam Meyers (CrowdStrike)

Institutions must continuously inventory and inspect software, accounts and partner access, and not let an 'AI' narrative distract from banks' own basic-security responsibility.Kim Myung-joo (Seoul Women's Univ.)

AI did not create a new attack method; it automated and scaled existing attack steps, so defenders should expect speed and volume rather than novelty.SK Shieldus

Weak handling of security basics — exposed side systems and weak access control — was the decisive gap, and firms with MFA and access controls withstood the same attacks. FSC Chair Lee Eog-weon said banks were 'very lacking even in the very basic parts.'Weekly Kyunghyang analysis

Oct 23, 2026: the new Seoul Serious Crimes Investigation Office, notified by police on Oct 21, declined to open its own investigation, citing the advanced state of the police probe and ongoing international cooperation (Money Today). Police booked the case in early October under the Information and Communications Network Act with a 28-person team in four squads led by the cyber-terror response chief, later adding 15 forensics and international-cooperation staff, and are examining whether AI tools were used.
