What to do · Deepfakes

Is this video real? How to check a clip before you share it

Also called deepfakes, AI-generated video, synthetic media or 'cheapfakes' when real footage is edited.

Reviewed by Colin Balfe on Oct 27, 2026 · Updated Oct 27, 2026 · Every fact links to its source
If it's happening right now

Don't share it yet. Find where it first appeared, search whether major news outlets or fact-checkers have confirmed it, and check who posted it. If it's about voting, get the facts only from your state or local election office or vote.gov.

You can't reliably spot a good deepfake by looking at it. The checks that work are about where a clip came from and who has confirmed it, not about pixels. They take a few minutes, and they work for election clips, celebrity 'endorsements' and videos of people you know.

What to do, step by step

  1. Pause before you share.If a clip makes you angry or shocked, slow down. That is exactly when fakes spread fastest.
  2. Find where it first came from.Ask who posted it first and where. A brand-new or anonymous account is a warning sign; the account behind the Talarico deepfake was created only a month earlier.
  3. Reverse-search a screenshot.Take a screenshot of a key frame and upload it to Google Images or TinEye to find the original and see whether it was edited.
  4. See what trusted outlets say.Leave the post and search for the claim. If something this big were real, several news outlets or fact-checkers would be reporting it, or the campaign would have confirmed it.
  5. Look and listen for glitches.Check whether lips match the words, and watch for odd blinking, warped edges or backgrounds, and a flat or slurred voice. Newer fakes may show none of these signs, so this is only one clue.
  6. Check for Content Credentials.Upload an image or paste its link into contentcredentials.org/verify to see any record of the tool used, including AI. A missing credential does not prove a file is fake.
  7. Read the fine print on ads.Check who paid for an ad (usually stated at the end) and look for an AI disclaimer. Many states require one, but most AI ads this year carried none.
  8. Get voting details only from officials.Don't click links in unexpected texts or emails about your registration. Check with your state or county election office instead.

Warning signs

It makes you furious or thrilled
Fakes are built to trigger a fast share. Strong emotion is the cue to slow down.
A brand-new or anonymous account posted it first
The account behind a deepfake of Texas Senate candidate James Talarico had been created about a month earlier.
No major outlet is reporting it
If a candidate really said something explosive, newsrooms would be covering it within hours.
It tells you to vote differently
A clip or message changing your polling place, date or method is a red flag. Check with your election office.
It's an ad with no 'paid for by' line, or no AI disclosure
Many states require AI disclosures in political ads, but most AI ads this cycle didn't carry one.

What not to rely on

Your eyes alone
Lip-sync errors, odd blinking and warped backgrounds are worth noticing, but the best fakes don't have them, and real videos can look glitchy.
AI 'deepfake detector' apps
Results vary widely and can be wrong in both directions. Treat them as one clue, never as proof.
The number of views or shares
Bots can inflate both; Bloomberg reports bot-boosted views on the Matryoshka operation's fake celebrity videos.
A verified badge or familiar name
Accounts can be bought, hacked or impersonated. Check the account's history and when it was created.

Where to report it

  • The platform where you saw it: use its report button for manipulated or misleading media
  • Your state or local election office: if it gives false voting information (find yours at vote.gov)
  • The FBI: if it's being used in a scam, such as fake donation appeals

Questions people ask

How can you tell if a video is a deepfake?

Don't rely on spotting glitches; good fakes don't have obvious ones. Check where the clip first appeared, whether major outlets or fact-checkers have confirmed it, and who posted it. A brand-new or anonymous account posting something explosive is a warning sign.

Can I check a video with a deepfake detector?

You can try, but detectors are unreliable and can be wrong in both directions. Use them as one clue at most. Where a clip came from, and who has confirmed it, matters more.

What are Content Credentials?

A labelling standard (C2PA) that some cameras, apps and AI tools use to record how an image or video was made. You can upload a file at contentcredentials.org/verify. If there's no record, that doesn't prove it's fake or real.

Are AI political ads legal?

In most states, yes, with conditions. NCSL counted 31 states regulating election deepfakes as of June 2026, most by requiring a disclosure. There's no federal law on AI in campaign ads; AI-voiced robocalls fall under federal robocall rules.

What should I do if I've already shared a fake?

Delete it, and post a short correction where you shared it. That stops it spreading further and helps the people who saw it from you.

Where do I report an election deepfake?

Report it on the platform. If it gives false voting information, tell your state or local election office.
