AI fakes in the 2026 midterms: the tracker

Updated Oct 27, 2026 · Election Day is Tuesday, Nov 3 · Every entry sourced
The short answer

14 AI-made election fakes and AI ads are on our record this cycle, 9 of them confirmed fake or taken down. Before you share a shocking clip of a candidate this week, run the checks below. For where and when to vote, use your state election office or vote.gov, not a video, text or call.

Before you share: how to check a clip

  1. Pause before you shareIf a clip makes you angry or shocked, slow down. That is exactly when fakes spread fastest. (source)
  2. Find where it first came fromAsk who posted it first and where. A brand-new or anonymous account is a warning sign; the account behind the Talarico deepfake was created only a month earlier. (source)
  3. Reverse-search a screenshotTake a screenshot of a key frame and upload it to Google Images or TinEye to find the original and see whether it was edited. (source)
  4. See what trusted outlets sayLeave the post and search for the claim. If something this big were real, several news outlets or fact-checkers would be reporting it, or the campaign would have confirmed it. (source)
  5. Look and listen for glitchesCheck whether lips match the words, and watch for odd blinking, warped edges or backgrounds, and a flat or slurred voice. Newer fakes may show none of these signs, so this is only one clue. (source)
  6. Check for Content CredentialsUpload an image or paste its link into contentcredentials.org/verify to see any record of the tool used, including AI. A missing credential does not prove a file is fake. (source)
  7. Read the fine print on adsCheck who paid for an ad (usually stated at the end) and look for an AI disclaimer. Many states require one, but most AI ads this year carried none. (source)
  8. Get voting details only from officialsDon't click links in unexpected texts or emails about your registration. Check with your state or county election office instead. (source)

  • Oct 24, 2026 alleged fake North Carolina 11th Congressional District (US House). It closes: 'Jamie Ager talks like you and me on TV, but he's dancing with they/them.' Made or posted by: Republican Jennifer Balkcom's campaign (approved the ad); paid for by the National Republican Congressional Committee. The rules

AI-voice robocalls fall under federal robocall law
The FCC ruled (adopted Feb 2, released Feb 8, 2024) that AI-generated voices count as 'artificial' voices under the Telephone Consumer Protection Act, so such calls need the called person's prior express consent. FCC Chair Rosenworcel said state attorneys general 'can go after the bad actors behind these robocalls'. (FCC Declaratory Ruling FCC 24-17)
Texas: no deceptive AI ads in the final 30 days
Texas's 2019 law bars AI deepfake political videos published within 30 days of an election with intent to influence it. Rep. Vikki Goodwin filed a criminal complaint under it against Lt. Gov. Dan Patrick's campaign in September. (FOX 4, Sep 22)
Mississippi: criminal penalties for election deepfakes
Section 97-13-47 of the Mississippi Code carries up to 5 years in prison and/or fines up to $10,000 when a deepfake is meant to deter someone from voting. The attorney general used it to force down a Senate campaign's AI video on Oct 23. (WLBT, Oct 23)
Colorado: label it or face civil penalties
Colorado's HB 24-1147 requires a clear disclosure on deepfakes of candidates, with exemptions for satire or parody. Its first complaint, over an AI video of state Sen. Cathy Kipp, is under investigation. (Colorado Politics, Oct 13)
Maryland: a label doesn't make it legal
A Maryland law in effect since June 1, 2026 bans certain deceptive election deepfakes even if labelled, with fines up to $5,000 and up to five years in prison. In September a federal judge temporarily blocked Montana's deepfake law on First Amendment grounds. (The Conversation via Cobb Courier, Sep 30)

Voting: the only sources to trust

  • Check your registration at vote.gov vote.gov is an official US government website for registering, checking your registration or updating it in your state. The FBI's Oct 15 alert recommends confirming your status there rather than through a link in a text or email. (FBI IC3, Oct 15)
  • Find your own election official at CanIVote.org The National Association of Secretaries of State's #TrustedInfo2026 campaign points voters to their state and local election officials, reachable through its nonpartisan site CanIVote.org. (NASS #TrustedInfo2026)
  • Verify any call or text saying your polling place moved Don't trust a call, text or email about where or how to vote without checking it with your local election office. In a 2020 example, Oklahoma's state election board secretary said officials 'will never contact you by text message to tell you your polling place has changed'. Report suspected voter intimidation to 866-OUR-VOTE. (Consumer Reports, Oct 28, 2020)

Questions people ask

How can I tell if a political video is a deepfake?

Don’t rely on spotting glitches. Check where it first appeared, whether a major outlet or the campaign has confirmed it, and whether fact-checkers have rated it. A brand-new or anonymous account posting an explosive clip is a warning sign.

Are AI political ads legal?

In most states, yes, with conditions. Many states now require AI-generated political ads to carry a disclosure, and a few ban certain deceptive deepfakes close to an election. There is no federal law covering AI in campaign ads; AI-voiced robocalls are covered by federal robocall rules.

What do I do if I see an election deepfake?

Don’t share it. Report it on the platform where you saw it, and if it gives false voting information, report it to your state or local election office.

Where can I check where and when to vote?

Your state or local election office, or vote.gov. Treat any call, text or video that changes your polling place or date with suspicion and check it with your election office.
