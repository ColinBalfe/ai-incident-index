Is this a scam? · Deepfake doctor or health-expert ad for supplements and 'cures'

A real doctor seems to recommend a miracle pill. Did they?

Reviewed by Colin Balfe on Oct 27, 2026 · Updated Oct 27, 2026 · Every fact links to its source
The short answer

Almost certainly fake if a doctor in a social media ad or video promises a cure, a GLP-1 'alternative' or a reason to quit your medication. Check with your own doctor before buying anything, and never stop prescribed treatment because of a video.

The check

Answer these honestly. A single "yes" to a warning sign is enough to stop.

  • Is a doctor promising a cure, or results that sound too good to be true?The FTC says products claiming to cure many unrelated diseases won't, and the FDA lists 'miraculous cure', 'quick fix' and 'lowers blood sugar' among health fraud red flags.
  • Is the endorsement only in an ad or a new account?AARP suggests searching for the doctor and checking their official website for the endorsement, and checking whether the posting account is new or full of other scam content.
  • Is it telling you to stop or skip a prescribed medicine?The FTC warns not to stop or delay proven treatments. Deepfakes in Australia told patients to stop taking metformin.
  • Is the product sold outside a pharmacy, as an 'alternative' to a prescription drug?The FTC says supplements claiming to be alternatives to FDA-approved drugs are a red flag, and the AMA says to be skeptical of products not sold through a pharmacy or prescribed by your doctor.
  • Does the face or voice look or sound slightly wrong?Watch for lips out of sync, odd edges around the face, stiff expressions and an overly polished or robotic voice. But good fakes pass.
  • Are there patient testimonials and a 'money-back guarantee'?The FDA says some testimonials come from actors posing as patients or doctors, and lists 'no-risk money back guarantee' as a red flag.

If it's happening right now

Don't buy and don't change any medication. Ask your own doctor or pharmacist about the product. If you already ordered, check your card statement for repeat charges and call your card company. If you took it and feel unwell, contact a doctor right away.

How this scam works

You see a video, often on TikTok, Facebook, Instagram or YouTube, in which a well-known doctor or TV health expert seems to recommend a supplement or treatment.

The clip reuses real footage of the doctor, with AI changing their face, voice and words. Some 'doctors' are AI avatars who don't exist at all.

The product promises fast or miraculous results, such as weight loss, a 'natural Ozempic', or curing diabetes or high blood pressure, and may tell you to stop proven medicines.

A link takes you to a shop page, sometimes overseas, that often reappears under new names after reports.

You may get a product that doesn't work or contains undeclared ingredients, and may risk your health by delaying real treatment.

What AI changes

The American Medical Association's CEO wrote in 2026 that scammers use AI to clone real physicians' faces and voices without consent to sell supplements and counterfeit weight-loss pills. In Australia, deepfakes of Dr Norman Swan have pushed weight-loss products and told people to stop taking metformin.

What to do, step by step

  1. Keep taking your prescribed treatment.Don't stop or delay proven treatment because of an online video, the FTC says. Talk to your doctor first.
  2. Check with the real doctor or your own.The AMA suggests contacting your doctor's office if in doubt. Search for the doctor named in the ad and look on their official site.
  3. If you bought something, watch your card.Call your card company to dispute charges you didn't agree to, and see the FTC's 'What To Do if You Were Scammed'.
  4. If you had side effects, get care and report it.Contact a doctor right away, then report it to the FDA's MedWatch program or at 1-800-FDA-1088 (1-800-332-1088), the FTC says.
  5. Report the ad.Report it on the platform where you saw it, and to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Warn friends and family who may have seen it.

Real cases

  • Aug 2026 Australia. The Australian Medical Association took its campaign against medical deepfakes to the Prime Minister. Deepfakes of Dr Norman Swan had promoted weight-loss products and told people to stop taking metformin for almost two years. (Medical Republic)
  • Apr 2026 United States. New Orleans physician Dr Maurice Sholas found AI-generated videos of himself selling vitamins on TikTok. Platforms largely ignored his reports, and a friend's husband bought the product believing it was him. (AARP The Perfect Scam)
  • Dec 2025 United Kingdom. Fact-checkers Full Fact found deepfake TikTok videos of real doctors and experts, including a University of Liverpool professor, promoting menopause supplements. The supplement company said the accounts were unaffiliated, and TikTok removed them after a 'moderation error'. (The Independent via AOL)

Where to report it

  • The platform where you saw the ad: use the report button on the ad, video or account
  • The FTC: ReportFraud.ftc.gov
  • Side effects from a product: FDA MedWatch, 1-800-FDA-1088 (1-800-332-1088)
  • The doctor who was impersonated: contact their practice or hospital through its official website

Questions people ask

Would a real doctor sell supplements in a social media ad?

Treat it as fake unless the doctor's own verified website or channels confirm it. The AMA says scammers clone physicians' faces and voices without consent to sell supplements and weight-loss pills.

Is there a 'natural Ozempic' or GLP-1 alternative?

Be very skeptical. Doctors have found AI videos promoting fake 'GLP-1 alternatives', and the FTC says supplements claiming to be alternatives to FDA-approved drugs are a red flag. Ask your doctor.

Can a supplement cure diabetes or high blood pressure?

The FTC says federal law bars promoting dietary supplements to treat disease, so such claims are false. The FDA lists 'lowers blood sugar' as a health fraud red flag.

I bought it. Is it dangerous?

It might be. The FDA warns weight-loss and similar products may contain hidden drugs or chemicals. Stop taking it, and talk to your doctor or pharmacist.

How do I report a deepfake health ad?

Report it on the platform, and to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. Reports from many users can help get it removed.
